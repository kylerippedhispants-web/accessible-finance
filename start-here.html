Choose one question and follow three steps. You can stop after any step, come back later, or open the extra detail in a guide when you need it. You do not need an account or money to invest to begin.

Spending and investing principles can apply widely. The account path below uses Canadian rules; U.S. readers can start with the U.S. account explainer.

Path 1 · Everyday money

Understand my spending

A budget is a way to see your choices, not a test of willpower. Start with one ordinary month and rough amounts you can refine.

  1. Learn a flexible approach to budgeting. Separate bills, irregular costs, and money you can use flexibly.
  2. Try the cash-flow tool. Enter an example first, or use your take-home income and expenses. The tool saves its entries in your browser.
  3. Explore a small recurring transfer. Only choose an amount that fits after essentials and debt payments; a zero-dollar start is still useful information.

Finish with: a one-month picture and one change to test. If expenses exceed income, use the picture to identify the shortfall before setting a savings target.

Path 2 · A cash buffer

Prepare for unexpected costs

A first buffer can be small. The aim is to make the next urgent expense easier to handle, without putting essential bills at risk.

  1. Learn how essentials and a reserve fit before investing. List one likely surprise cost and the cash you can access now.
  2. Understand what cash is for. Money needed at short notice needs access and stability, even when its growth is modest.
  3. Separate predictable costs from emergencies. Set aside money gradually for known annual bills, then choose a manageable first buffer target.

Finish with: a first target, a place to keep it accessible, and an amount to add when your budget allows. You can adjust all three as your circumstances change.

Path 3 · The fundamentals

Learn investing basics

Begin with what an investment owns and why it might provide a return. You do not need to choose a product to learn the ideas.

  1. Tell investing and speculation apart. Ask what is expected to create value and what could go wrong.
  2. Connect risk to the date you need the money. Price movement is only one risk; access, inflation, and permanent loss also matter.
  3. Use a simple decision framework. Name the goal, protect near-term needs, and compare costs before choosing an investment.

Finish with: a goal, a time horizon, and three questions you want answered before investing. Learning without buying anything is a valid next step.

Path 4 · Canada

Understand retirement accounts

An account sets the tax and access rules. The cash or investments inside it determine how the money behaves. Learn those two decisions separately.

  1. Meet the main Canadian registered accounts. Start with what each account is intended to do and who can use it.
  2. Compare a TFSA and an RRSP. Work through matching, tax rates, flexibility, and contribution room without assuming one answer fits everyone.
  3. Learn where CPP fits. Use a personal official estimate when planning; the maximum payment is not an amount everyone receives.

Finish with: a list of your relevant accounts, the records you need to check, and one unanswered question. When ready, explore a fictional retirement plan to see how assumptions change an estimate.
